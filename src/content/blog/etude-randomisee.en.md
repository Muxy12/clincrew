A randomised study is a trial in which the treatment assigned to each participant is decided by random draw. This simple principle is one of the most powerful in clinical research: it allows treatments to be compared fairly, without selection bias.

## Why randomise

If we chose who receives which treatment ourselves, we would introduce, intentionally or not, differences between the groups. [Randomisation](/en/glossaire/randomisation) spreads participants comparably: in the end, if one group fares better, it is attributable to the treatment, not to an initial imbalance.

## The value against bias

Randomisation often combines with [blinding](/en/glossaire/insu-aveugle) and a comparator, sometimes a [placebo](/en/glossaire/placebo). A randomised, double-blind, comparator-controlled trial is the standard for demonstrating a medicine's efficacy.

## A few variants

- **Parallel arms**: each group receives a different treatment, in parallel.
- **Cross-over**: each participant receives the compared treatments in turn.
- **Stratified randomisation**: groups are balanced on key factors (age, severity, etc.).

## Randomisation and monitoring

Compliance with the randomisation scheme is a point checked during [monitoring visits](/en/services/visites-de-monitoring): correct allocation, respect for blinding, traceability. A randomisation error is a deviation to document.

To understand a trial's whole design, see [randomisation, blinding and placebo](/en/blog/randomisation-insu-placebo).
