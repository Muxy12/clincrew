For a clinical trial to be reliable, bias must be limited. Three methods form the foundation of a comparative trial's design: randomisation, blinding and placebo. They combine so that the comparison between treatments is as fair as possible.

## Randomisation: comparing without selection bias

[Randomisation](/en/glossaire/randomisation) assigns treatment by random draw. It makes the groups comparable at the start and removes bias in treatment allocation.

## Blinding: neutralising assessment bias

[Blinding](/en/glossaire/insu-aveugle) hides the treatment received. In single blind, the participant does not know; in double blind, the assessing team does not know either. This limits bias, whether conscious or not.

## Placebo: measuring the real effect

The [placebo](/en/glossaire/placebo), a treatment with no active ingredient, serves as a comparator to distinguish the medicine's own effect from the effect of being treated. When a reference treatment exists, it is often used as the comparator.

## Why combine them

These three methods reinforce each other: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial is a standard for demonstrating efficacy. The choice is defined in the [protocol](/en/glossaire/protocole) of the [trial](/en/glossaire/essai-clinique).

## In short

- Randomisation: comparable groups, no selection bias.
- Blinding: objective assessment.
- Placebo: the medicine's real effect.
