Monitoring is one of the core activities of a clinical trial. It is organised around visits to investigator sites, each with a specific purpose. Understanding their role helps scope an assignment — and know what to expect from it.

## The four main types of visit

**Selection visit.** Before opening a site, its ability to run the study is assessed: experience of the investigator team, available patient population, facilities, compliance with good practice. The goal is to avoid opening a site that will not enrol or cannot follow the protocol.

**Initiation visit.** Just before start-up, the team is trained on the protocol, procedures and regulatory obligations. Essential documentation is checked and the site is confirmed ready to include the first patient.

**Monitoring (routine) visits.** During the study, the CRA checks data, patient safety and protocol compliance. This is the heart of the work: source data verification, follow-up of adverse events, management of deviations and corrective actions.

**Close-out visit.** At the end, documentation is finalised, materials and treatments are reconciled, and archiving is prepared.

## What risk-based monitoring changed

For a long time, monitoring relied on exhaustive data checking (100% SDV). The **ICH E6(R2)** addendum to Good Clinical Practice established a **risk-based** approach: focus effort on the data and processes that are **critical** to patient safety and result reliability, rather than checking everything the same way.

In practice, this means:

- identifying the study's major risks up front;
- combining on-site and centralised monitoring;
- adapting the frequency and intensity of visits to the actual level of risk.

For a CRA, that means less mechanical checking and more judgement: knowing where to look, and why.

## In short

Each visit addresses a stage of the study's life cycle — from selection to close-out. Well-run monitoring doesn't "tick boxes": it anticipates risks where they truly matter.
